## Lead Coach Operating Framework

The shared standard for preparing, leading and reviewing a TGC seminar.

## 1. How to Use This Guide

*A complete script and a practical operating manual*

Use it in three layers: Read the full explanation to understand the logic. Rehearse the delivery sequence to organise the room. Teach from the memory lines and participant questions rather than reading the document word for word.

This guide follows the structure of the seminar handouts. It combines the opening lecture, the practical movement blocks, the programming lecture and the technical reference material into one consistent Lead Coach document.

The Lead Coach is responsible for the standard of the seminar, but not for speaking continuously. Use demonstrations, participant observation, partner practice, tactile spotting, questions and short reflection tasks so that the group repeatedly moves between explanation and experience.

The material is intentionally detailed. A new Lead Coach may use the complete speaker notes. An experienced Lead Coach may work mainly from the module summaries, practical sequences and non-negotiable standards.

## 2. The Course Standard

*What must remain consistent across every seminar*

Movement standard: Eccentric and concentric phases should show the same positional quality. A participant does not demonstrate mastery when one phase is controlled and the other is survived.

| Course idea | Meaning for the Lead Coach | Visible seminar behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Mastery over RX | The target is effective use of motor potential, not completion at any cost. | Praise shape, effort, control and adaptation. Do not reward compromised standards simply because the movement was completed. |
| Static before dynamic | Own the position and the apparatus before adding speed or instability. | Return to floor shapes, holds, support positions and controlled apparatus work whenever dynamic positions break. |
| Strict before kipping | Kipping increases intensity; it does not replace prerequisite strength. | Use strict, isometric and eccentric work to prepare the athlete for dynamic force. |
| Individual intervention | Cues and constraints are tools, not universal solutions. | Identify whether the limiter is mobility, strength or coordination before choosing an intervention. |
| Spotting as scaling | Spotting preserves the intended position and provides sensory information. | Spot the athlete rather than the apparatus and progressively reduce assistance as control improves. |
| Training vs competition | Training builds adaptation; competition tests output. | Coach the higher standard in training and educate participants about predictable breakdown under fatigue. |

## 3. Lead Coach Responsibilities

*Before, during and after the seminar*

| Phase | Lead Coach responsibility | Assistant coach use |
|---|---|---|
| Before | Know the content, choose personal examples, prepare the whiteboard, confirm equipment and agree demonstrations, spotting and station ownership. | Walk the floor, test equipment heights, practise spotting positions, confirm who demonstrates each standard and who watches the room. |
| Opening | Create psychological safety, clarify participation expectations and establish the course language and standard. | Model engagement, learn names, observe movement history and note injuries or apprehension shared by participants. |
| Practical blocks | Protect the learning objective, time and safety standard while coaching the people in front of you. | Run lanes, spot, identify individual limiters, give the Lead Coach information and prevent participants from waiting unnecessarily. |

| Closing | Translate the weekend into programming and long-term coaching decisions. | Collect questions, support practical examples and note areas requiring team review. |
| After | Debrief the seminar: timing, safety, participation, questions, movement standards and improvements for the next delivery. | Share observations from stations and identify inconsistencies in language, demonstration or spotting. |

## 7. Delivery Pattern for Every Practical Block

*A repeatable seminar rhythm*

| 1. Frame | State the movement, the purpose and one learning outcome. |
|---|---|
| 2. Observe | Use a silent demonstration or participant attempt before giving the answer. |
| 3. Define the standard | Show the points of performance and the force direction. |
| 4. Experience | Let participants feel the position through floor work, apparatus work and spotting. |
| 5. Identify the limiter | Decide whether the primary problem is mobility, strength or coordination. |
| 6. Intervene | Choose the smallest useful cue, constraint, regression, contraction type or tactile reference. |
| 7. Re-test | Check whether the movement changed; do not assume the intervention worked. |
| 8. Close and transfer | Summarise the principle and connect it to coaching, programming or the next module. |

Lead Coach memory line: Do not demonstrate ten drills. Demonstrate a process that allows coaches to judge drills, create progressions and make decisions.

## 8. Coaching Language and Group Management

*The Lead Coach sets both the movement standard and the mindset standard*

| Use | Avoid | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| "Show me one rep with mastery." | "Just get the rep." | The first rewards quality and effort; the second rewards completion at any cost. |
| "What changed when you slowed down?" | "Try harder." | The question develops awareness; effort alone may reinforce the same fault. |
| "Can you feel contact through the whole foot?" | A long anatomical explanation during movement. | A sensory anchor is easier to act on in real time. |
| "This is the standard. We will adjust the task, not remove the standard." | Different technical standards for different ability levels. | Load, lever, range and assistance may change while the target position remains clear. |
| Specific praise for control, effort, patience and improved shape. | Praise only for RX, speed or completion. | Lead Coach language teaches participants what the course values. |

### Understanding Gymnastics

Before we talk about the different types of gymnastics we see today, let's look at its origin.

Gymnastics can be traced back to the Ancient Greeks. They performed gymnastics alongside intellectual activities, believing that this combination helped align body and mind.

The Athenian Olympic Games are the first reference to structured gymnastics competition.

The word gymnastics comes from gymnazein, meaning naked exercise, as activities were performed without equipment, using only the body.

### Gymnastics Through History

The Romans later implemented gymnastics as part of their military training. Similar to how we use gymnastics today, they saw its value in strengthening both body and mind - but primarily for war.

They prevented further ancient Olympic Games and used gymnastics strictly as a military tool.

Fast forward several centuries, gymnastics became structured training again through the German educator Friedrich Ludwig Jahn, as part of physical education in schools.

Due to its development in schools and clubs across Europe, artistic gymnastics returned to the Olympic Games in 1896.

### Types Of Gymnastics

There are several types of gymnastics:

Artistic gymnastics, rhythmic gymnastics, acrobatic gymnastics, stunt work, and others.

Artistic gymnastics is the one most people are familiar with. It is an Olympic sport for both men and women, with different apparatuses.

Male apparatuses - how many are there? - Six.

### Men Vs Women - Apparatus Differences

Some people say it's because men are stronger than women. That's a very simplified and somewhat sexist explanation.

### Gymnastics & Crossfit Apparatus

- High bar.

- Rings.

- Parallel bars.

Parallel bars were introduced relatively recently to the CrossFit Games. During the 2022 Games, we saw them for the first time.

Yes - because athletes had exposure.

But did they move efficiently? Not really.

Let me give you a common gymnastics test - the Man Test on parallel bars.

You start on one side of the bars:

- 1 dip, travel to the other side

- 1 dip, travel back

- 2-2, 3-3, 4-4, 5-5

Now add one rule:

- elbows must stay locked

- arms supinated during straight-arm travel

If elbows bend, the athlete must come down and perform a 200 m run.

Because within CrossFit we train volume, but often forget about holds.

If athletes haven't trained a virtuous, supinated elbow lockout and built an isometric base, they struggle to stabilise the joint. They compensate with muscular force, fatigue faster, and eventually bend the elbow.

The supinated lockout creates torque around the elbow, stabilising the joint and preventing flexion.

If this isn't trained, force production increases, negative tension builds, and under fatigue the elbow bends.

Example: Superman Test - Parallel Bars

### Role Of The Strength & Conditioning Coach

CrossFit is a strength and conditioning program, and we are strength and conditioning coaches.

### Form, Strength & Control

**In other words:**

- we need strength to perform a movement with proper form,

- we need proper form to move with body awareness and control.

If we reverse it:

- to move with body awareness and control, we need proper form,

- to move with proper form, we need prerequisite strength.

### Introduction To Mastery

Now I will introduce mastery.

Virtuosity is defined as performing the common uncommonly well.

Mastery is defined as:

The improvement of an athlete's skill to effectively use their motor potential.

To present mastery in movement, we must have:

- body awareness,

- body control,

- proper form.

To achieve proper form, we must have strength through the full range of motion - start to finish.

Any lack of strength through a range of motion compromises position and sends forces in the wrong direction.

### Eccentric Vs Concentric Control

We know we are stronger eccentrically than concentrically.

But when moving with mastery, the eccentric and concentric phases should look the same.

Example: Back squat

You may see an athlete control the descent perfectly, but struggle to hold position on the way up.

That athlete is not strong enough to move that weight with mastery.

Same example: strict pull-up

If positions break on the way up but look fine on the way down, the athlete lacks strength for mastery.

### Strict Before Kipping

If we cannot control strict movements, we will struggle with kipping movements.

This brings us to our second rule:

> RULE #2 - Strict before kipping

(or: strict strength-biased training)

Newton's third law tells us:

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

If forces are sent in the wrong direction, reactions follow.

In a kipping pull-up:

- bent knees,

- lack of tension in arch,

- kicking legs forward

Where do the forces go? - Backwards.

We then pull toward the bar, push away again, and end up with a large, inefficient swing.

But where do we want to go? - Up.

### Prerequisites For Kipping

I say one.

One strict pull-up - where concentric and eccentric look the same.

**That shows:**

- strength,

- proper form,

- body awareness,

- control.

This leads to a more efficient kipping movement, higher intensity, and reduced injury risk.

Picking up right after strict-before-kipping.

### Tool #1 - Cues

The first tool is cues.

We tell people what to do differently:

- knees out,

- hips back,

- chest up.

You've all heard these cues.

- Sometimes cues work.

- Sometimes they don't.

### Tool #2 - Constraints

If cues don't work, we can use constraints.

**For example:**

- air squats facing a wall,

- limiting range of motion,

- changing stance or grip.

Constraints force the athlete to work harder to find better positions.- „example being relentless"

But if the athlete:

- doesn't understand the cue,

- doesn't have the mobility,

- or doesn't have the strength,

they still won't move better.

### Tool #3 - Interventions

That's when we need interventions.

**Interventions address:**

- mobility,

- stability,

- and strength.

If we only mobilise, we leave the joint unstable.

If we only strengthen, we may reinforce bad positions.

It has to be individual.

### Individual Examples

Example one:

A yoga practitioner joins your gym.

Passive range of motion is excellent.

But when asked to perform an air squat, the ankles, knees, and hips don't move together.

Because they lack stability and strength.

So for them, we prioritise:

- joint stability,

- strength through range.

Example two: A powerlifter.

They can squat heavy with a barbell, but struggle with an air squat.

Because of limited mobility and a poor length-tension relationship.

For them, we prioritise:

- mobility,

- increasing usable range of motion.

By improving range, we also improve strength - because the contractile filaments overlap more optimally.

### Muscle Contractions

To understand movement development better, we need to understand muscle contractions.

**There are:**

- isometric contractions,

- concentric contractions,

- eccentric contractions.

An isometric contraction happens when the muscle is under tension but does not change length.

Example: chin-over-bar hold.

A concentric contraction happens when the muscle shortens under load.

Example: pulling up.

An eccentric contraction happens when the muscle lengthens under load.

Example: lowering from the bar.

### Isometrics In Crossfit

All the time.

Not often enough.

But if you remember the 100 words of fitness, it tells us to train holds.

### Rule #3 - Build An Isometric Base

Isometric contractions are essential for:

- joint stabilisation,

- connective tissue development,

- neuromuscular adaptations.

Our standard:

~2 minutes (120 seconds) of isometric capacity per position.

**This applies to:**

- gymnastics,

- weightlifting,

- strongman,

- and more.

### Isometric Examples

**Example:**

- Hollow hold on the floor - 30 seconds

- Hollow hang on the bar - 10 seconds

In kipping pull-ups, shapes usually break down after about 10 seconds.

That's why athletes often perform only 3-5 quality reps before positions deteriorate.

**Goal:**

- 2 minutes hollow on the floor,

- then 2 minutes hollow on the bar.

When shapes improve, force production improves.

### Isometrics & Weightlifting

Think about the snatch.

**Can your athlete:**

- hold the top of an overhead squat?

- hold the bottom position?

- maintain those positions with a PVC or empty bar for 2 minutes?

If not, consider:

- movement ability,

- positional strength,

- cognitive awareness.

### Benefits Of Isometrics

**Isometric training is:**

- safe and low impact,

- beneficial for connective tissue,

- helpful for flexibility,

- improves coordination,

- increases force production,

- reduces injury risk,

- supports bone density,

- and more.

### Eccentric Training

Now let's talk about eccentric contractions.

Eccentric training is undervalued in CrossFit.

**During eccentric contractions:**

- muscles lengthen under load,

- energy is absorbed,

- shock is reduced.

This is important for:

- rehabilitation,

- tendinopathies,

- aging populations,

- strength development.

### Muscle Architecture Adaptations

Eccentric loading leads to:

- increased cross-sectional area (hypertrophy),

- increased fascicle length,

- improved length-tension relationship,

- changes in pennation angle,

- improved motor unit recruitment.

You don't need to know this in detail -

but understanding why training works improves adherence and outcomes.

### Motor Units

A motor unit consists of:

- one nerve,

- and multiple muscle fibres.

Muscle fibres follow the all-or-nothing principle:

They contract 100% or not at all.

**Strength increases by:**

- recruiting more motor units,

- synchronising their activation.

Eccentric overload helps improve this synchronisation.

### Rule #4 - Use Eccentrics For Development

Use eccentric loading as a strength and positional development tool

### Sickness - Wellness - Fitness Continuum

You've all seen this before.

CrossFit uses the sickness-wellness-fitness continuum to explain health.

We can also use it to explain movement quality breakdown.

### Karen Example

Let's take Karen - 150 wall balls for time.

**A Games athlete:**

- finishes under 4 minutes,

- maintains mastery,

A well-trained recreational athlete:

- finishes in 5-7 minutes,

Our responsibility is to scale appropriately.

Yes.

Example:

**Instead of 150 wall balls:**

**30-30-30-30-30 reps**

- decreasing weight each round

This preserves mastery while increasing intensity.

**Later:**

- progress to 50-50-50,

- 75-75,

- 100-50,

- then retest 150.

This prepares athletes for competitions where wall balls are followed by heavy lifts.

M - Mobilise

P - Potentiate

Potentiation primes the nervous system so that:

- force production improves

- coordination improves

- movement quality improves

This is where performance is enhanced.

### Lever Systems - Required Addition

The body functions as a lever system.

**First-Class Lever**

- Fulcrum between force and load

**Example: neck extension**

**Fulcrum: atlanto-occipital joint**

**Load: head**

- Force: posterior neck muscles

**Second-Class Lever**

- Load between fulcrum and force

**Example: calf raise**

**Fulcrum: toes**

**Load: body weight**

- Force: Achilles tendon

Third-Class Lever (Most of the body)

- Force between fulcrum and load

**Example: biceps curl**

**Fulcrum: elbow**

**Force: biceps**

- Load: forearm + weight

Hollow = Largest Lever

In hollow, the body becomes a long third-class lever, which:

- increases torque demands

- magnifies weakness

- exposes inefficiencies immediately

### Anthropometrics - Why Pistols Look Different

### Asis - Anterior Superior Iliac Spine

The ASIS is a bony landmark on the pelvis:

- origin for the sartorius

- attachment point for the inguinal ligament

- reference point for pelvic orientation

**Functionally:**

- ASIS position influences femur angle

- affects knee tracking

- determines Q-angle magnitude

When we talk about squat mechanics, pelvis position matters - and ASIS gives us a reference.

### Q-Angle - Critical For Pistols

The Q-Angle (Quadriceps Angle) represents the combined line of pull of:

- the quadriceps

- and the patellar tendon

Defined by two lines:

**ASIS -> centre of patella**

- Centre of patella -> tibial tuberosity

**Average values:**

**Men: ~10-12°**

- Women: ~14-16°

**Women typically have:**

- wider pelvis

- more laterally positioned ASIS

- increased Q-angle

### Why Q-Angle Matters In Squats

**A higher Q-angle increases:**

- lateral pull on the patella

- valgus torque at the knee

- ACL loading under dynamic conditions

**In pistols:**

- load is unilateral

- valgus moments increase

- margin for error is small

This is anatomy, not a coaching flaw.

So again:

Should everyone squat the same? No.

### ACL - Why Valgus Is A Problem

**The ACL (Anterior Cruciate Ligament) resists:**

- anterior tibial translation

- excessive tibial rotation

- valgus collapse under load

In pistols, ACL stress increases when:

- knee collapses medially

- hip internally rotates uncontrollably

- trunk shifts laterally

- foot loses arch integrity

**High-risk scenarios:**

- fatigue

- speed

- load

- combined flexion + rotation

This is why valgus habits under speed matter - especially in open workouts, lunges, step-ups, and pistols.

### Biomechanical Principles Applied To The Pistol Squat

1. Base of Support

Single foot -> reduced stability -> higher balance demand

1. Centre of Mass (COM)

COM must stay over the foot. Long femur or long torso increases forward COM shift.

**3. Levers & Moment Arms**

- Longer femur -> longer hip moment arm

- More torque required at hip and knee

- Increased trunk lean is a mechanical solution, not a fault

1. Torque

**Torque = Force x Moment Arm Unilateral loading increases torque at:**

- hip

- knee

- ankle

1. Joint Coupling

Ankle, knee, and hip must move together. Restriction at one joint forces compensation elsewhere.

1. Force Vectors

Ground reaction force must travel:

- from foot -> knee -> hip -> trunk

If alignment is lost:

- forces shift laterally

- ligament stress increases

- efficiency decreases

1. Eccentric Control

Eccentric strength controls descent. Lack of it leads to:

- collapse

- bouncing

- joint stress

Intervention: eccentric calf loading

The push-up is not a "basic exercise". It is a closed-chain horizontal press that requires the athlete to manage:

- body mass as the load

- a long lever system

- scapulothoracic rhythm

- trunk stability

- and force transfer from hands to feet

If any link in that chain fails, the movement breaks.

### Biomechanical Framework Of The Push-Up

1. Closed-Chain Mechanics

Hands are fixed. The body moves around them.

**This increases:**

- joint compression (good for stability)

- proprioceptive demand

- coordination between joints

Closed-chain pressing is safer only if alignment is respected.

1. Lever Length & Load

The push-up is a third-class lever:

- fulcrum: shoulder joint

- force: triceps + chest

- load: body mass

The longer the body (straight legs, feet elevated):

- the longer the lever

- the higher the torque at the shoulder and elbow

This is why regressions must shorten the lever, not break positions.

1. Centre of Mass (COM)

In a proper push-up:

- COM stays between hands and feet

- trunk moves as one rigid unit

Sagging hips or piked hips shift COM and:

- increase shoulder stress

- reduce pressing efficiency

### Position A - Top Of The Push-Up

Points of Performance (POP)

From bottom to top:

- toes

- knees extended

- posterior pelvic tilt

- ribcage down

- scapular protraction

- elbows locked

- hands slightly turned out

**Biomechanical purpose:**

- protraction engages serratus anterior

- ribcage down prevents lumbar extension

- PPT aligns pelvis under trunk

This is a support position, not a rest.

### Position B - Bottom Of The Push-Up

Eccentric Control Is the Key

**The eccentric phase:**

- absorbs force

- controls joint alignment

- reveals strength deficits

POP at the bottom:

- toes + knees extended

**PPT maintained**

- ribcage still down

- scapula retracts naturally

- elbows track close to body

- reduces shoulder internal rotation torque

- improves triceps contribution

- stabilises scapula

### Eccentric = Concentric = Mastery

**If an athlete:**

- drops fast

- bounces

- or loses trunk tension

They lack eccentric strength, not motivation.

**Eccentric control:**

- protects connective tissue

- improves motor learning

- increases long-term strength

### Scapular Biomechanics

Push-ups require scapulothoracic rhythm.

- Protraction at the top

- Controlled retraction on the way down

- Smooth transition, no "locking"

**Common error:**

- fixed scapula -> shoulder overload

### Common Faults & Biomechanical Causes

**Sagging Hips**

**loss of PPT**

- weak anterior core

- poor force transfer

**Pike Position**

- athlete shortens lever to reduce load

- compensation, not control

**Elbows Flaring**

- increased shoulder torque

- poor triceps contribution

- destabilised scapula

### Variations - Biomechanical Intent

- Diamond -> increases elbow flexion torque

- Wide -> increases shoulder horizontal abduction

- Long lever -> increases total torque

**Staggered -> asymmetrical load**

- Maltese / Archer -> extreme unilateral torque

Each variation changes lever length and moment arms.

### Scaling - What To Do And What To Avoid

**❌ Knees on Floor**

- shortens lever

- but breaks trunk alignment

- poor transfer to full push-up

**✅ Box Push-Ups**

- preserves alignment

- adjusts lever angle

- scalable over time

**✅ Barbell in Rig**

- precise lever manipulation

- excellent for progression

- a change in force direction

- under unstable conditions

- while maintaining joint centration

- through a large range of motion

STRICT RING MUSCLE-UP (sRMU)

WHY sRMU IS THE TRUE FOUNDATION

**The strict RMU develops:**

- straight-arm pulling strength

- deep shoulder extension control

- scapular depression under load

- force transfer without momentum

This is why:

sRMU is not about "getting over the rings"

it is about owning every position

BIOMECHANICS OF THE sRMU

Phase 1 - Strict Pull (Below the Rings)

**Key demands:**

- scapular depression

- lat engagement

- elbow tracking close to body

- ribcage control

**Biomechanics:**

- force vector is mostly vertical

- rings must stay close to the body

- elbows travel down and back, not wide

**If elbows flare:**

- lat contribution decreases

- biceps dominate

- shoulder destabilizes

Phase 2 - Transition (The Critical Zone)

> This is not a separate skill.

It is a change in force direction.

**What changes:**

- pulling down becomes pressing down

- elbows move from below -> above the rings

- shoulder moves into deep extension

**Biomechanical demand:**

- scapula must stay depressed

- shoulder must remain centered

- wrists must tolerate load in flexion

This is where most athletes fail - not due to lack of strength, but due to:

- poor shoulder extension tolerance

- lack of time under tension in this range

Phase 3 - Strict Dip (Above the Rings)

Key points:

- elbows stay close

- scapula remains depressed

- trunk stays organized

This phase is mechanically similar to:

- ring dips

- but only if the transition was controlled

If the athlete "jumps" the transition:

- dip becomes unstable

- shoulders compensate

- elbows flare

### Why Eccentrics Matter So Much

**Eccentric RMUs:**

- increase tissue tolerance

- improve positional awareness

- lengthen and strengthen shoulder extensors

**Biomechanically:**

- eccentric loading increases motor unit recruitment

- connective tissue adapts more slowly than muscle

- this phase prepares the shoulder for kipping forces later

This is injury prevention through biomechanics, not caution.

KIPPING RING MUSCLE-UP (kRMU)

### What Defines A Kipping Movement

A kipping movement is:

an aggressive change in direction and speed

Kipping does not remove strength requirements -

it amplifies force demands.

If positions are not owned strictly, kipping magnifies errors.

### Ring Swing - The True Prerequisite

A good RMU starts with a good ring swing.

**Arch Position**

- hands slightly turned out

- elbows straight

- scapula active

- spinal extension with abdominal engagement

- knees together

Purpose:

- store elastic energy

- preload tissues

- prepare stretch-shortening cycle

**Hollow Position**

- press rings away

- ribs down

- hips open

- scapula protracts slightly

Purpose:

- redirect force

- prevent chasing the rings

- keep rings close to the body

### Force Direction - The Key Difference

**In ineffective kRMU:**

- athlete pulls up

- then throws chest forward

- then hopes to get over

**In effective kRMU:**

- athlete presses down on the rings

- reaction force moves body up

- elbows rotate around naturally

This is Newton's 3rd Law in action:

Push down -> reaction goes up

### Amortization Phase (Why Timing Matters)

Between arch and hollow:

- elastic energy is stored

- the quicker the transition

- the more force is returned

If the athlete pauses:

- energy is lost

- strength demand spikes

- compensation occurs

**This is why:**

- tension must be present before the transition

- not created during it

**Cause:**

- asymmetric force production

- uneven shoulder stability

- one side searching for leverage

**Biomechanics:**

- athlete rotates to reduce load on weaker side

Rings Drifting Wide

**Cause:**

- lack of lat compression

- poor scapular depression

Result:

- longer lever

- higher shoulder torque

- stalled transition

Jumping Too Early

**Cause:**

- impatience

- lack of strict capacity

Result:

- bypassed strength development

- unstable dip

- shoulder overload

**PREREQUISITES FOR kRMU (NON-NEGOTIABLE)**

- strict ring dips with mastery

- strict chest-to-ring pull-ups

**eccentric RMUs controlled**

- ring swing with shape integrity

If these are missing:

- kRMU becomes a gamble, not a progression

WHY sRMU -> kRMU IS A LAW, NOT A PREFERENCE

**Strict work:**

- builds positional strength

- teaches force direction

- prepares connective tissue

**Kipping:**

- increases intensity

- increases fatigue

- increases injury risk if misapplied

> This is not ideology.

This is mechanics + biology.

### Final Takeaway - RMU Coaching Through Biomechanics

The ring muscle-up is not:

- a pulling problem

- or a bravery problem

**If you coach:**

### Unit Objectives (Set The Frame)

By the end of this lecture, you should:

1. Understand the risks associated with different training methods - and how to manage them responsibly

1. Understand the programming rules that apply specifically to gymnastics training

1. Understand strength adaptations and the contraindications of concurrent training systems, and why CrossFit is designed as one workout per day

Programming is where science, art, and craft meet.

Or as Andrew Hunt puts it:

"Programming is the art of organizing complexity into something that works."

### What Are We Programming For?

To understand programming, we must first understand what we are trying to achieve.

For general population members, we are programming for fitness.

CrossFit defines fitness as:

constantly varied functional movements performed at high intensity.

Mathematically simplified:

### Cf = Cv + Fm @ Hi

So the question becomes:

### Power, Strength & Force Production

In a workout, output is expressed as:

- speed

- strength at speed

-> power

Power = Force x Distance ÷ Time

To move faster or generate more power, we must:

- increase force production

- without losing distance (ROM)

- or increasing time unnecessarily

This is why strength development is non-negotiable.

But - and this is critical -

strength alone is not enough.

### Can We Be Too Strong? (Context Matters)

Yes - if we ignore the variables.

Example:

A 100 m sprinter runs 11 seconds with a stride length of 2.1 m.

They increase maximal strength aggressively, but:

- muscle architecture adapts

- sarcomeres shorten

- ROM decreases

Stride length drops to 1.8 m.

Result:

**force ↑**

### Rom ↓

- speed stays the same or decreases

Job role #2 failed: performance did not improve.

So the real goal is:

Increase strength while preserving or improving usable range of motion.

This is where gymnastics matters.

### Why Gymnastics Belongs In Crossfit

**Gymnastics trains:**

- balance

- coordination

- accuracy

- agility

These are neurological qualities - not just muscular ones.

Examples:

- Elderly athletes -> balance & coordination (fall prevention, osteoporosis)

- Adolescents -> accuracy & motor learning during growth

- Competitive athletes -> proprioception under fatigue

This is why gymnastics programming is not optional.

It is foundational.

### Understanding Risk - Not Fear, But Responsibility

Training always carries risk.

Our job is not to avoid it -

our job is to understand and manage it.

### Isometric Contractions - Risk & Value

Benefits:

- joint stability

- positional strength

- neurological efficiency

**Risk:**

- increased blood pressure

**This is why:**

- the coach-athlete relationship matters

- screening matters

- awareness matters

We treat high blood pressure like a red flag, not a suggestion.

### Eccentric Contractions - Powerful & Dangerous If Mismanaged

Benefits:

- increased cross-sectional area

- increased fascicle length

- improved pennation angle

- strong neurological adaptations

**Risk:**

- exercise-induced rhabdomyolysis

**Risk increases with:**

- low training age

- high volume

- hot environments

- dehydration

- illness

- genetic factors

Guideline for GPP:

👉 Do not exceed ~125 seconds of purposeful eccentric loading per session.

This aligns perfectly with:

- our 120 seconds isometric guideline

Memorable. Practical. Safe.

### Concentric Contractions - The Silent Accumulation

**Risk:**

- joint wear and tear

- synovial stress over time

Ask yourself:

Volume matters - especially long term.

### Programming Rules (Non-Negotiable)

### Rule 1

Gymnastics supplements the CrossFit program - it does not replace it.

Gymnastics may appear as:

- warm-up

- mobility

- stabilisation

- strength development

But it does not hijack the day's primary stimulus.

Frequent exposure is most effective.

Some form of bodyweight movement should appear daily.

**This improves:**

- awareness

- motor skill retention

- transfer to all other modalities

### Rule 3

Standardised gymnastics warm-ups are best practice.

- increased exposure

- improved coach's eye

- earlier identification of faults

Consistency builds clarity.

### Rule 4

Train gymnastics first.

- central nervous system fatigue

**Once CNS fatigue sets in:**

- quality motor unit recruitment drops

- we stop training positions

- we start rehearsing bad patterns

Gymnastics is about precision, not exhaustion.

When writing gymnastics programs, consider:

- Intensity -> % of max or time under tension

**Volume -> total reps or total TUT**

**Frequency -> must progress logically**

**Recovery -> must be scientific**

- Specificity -> train what you want to improve

**Overload -> gradual and intentional**

- SACCTS -> strength adaptation & contraindications of concurrent training systems

- regression

- frustration

SACCTS - course explanation: Strength work and high-volume endurance or metabolic work create different adaptive demands. When competing systems are layered too closely, neural quality and the intended strength adaptation may be reduced. The course therefore places gymnastics strength and skill while the nervous system is fresh and treats one primary workout per day as the default structure.

|---|---|---|
| Intensity | Percentage of maximum, difficulty of lever or time under tension. | Reduce reps and raise positional demand for high-intensity strength work. |
| Volume | Total repetitions or total time under tension. | Accumulate quality without allowing the standard to deteriorate. |
| Frequency | How often the athlete is exposed to the movement. | Use frequent small exposures rather than rare exhausting sessions. |
| Recovery | Time needed for tissue, nervous system and skill quality to recover. | Separate competing demands and avoid adding every extra skill after the main workout. |
| Specificity | The adaptation follows the task trained. | Train the position, contraction type and apparatus that the athlete needs. |
| Overload | The demand increases gradually. | Add seconds, a small number of reps, lever length or reduced assistance. |
| FITT | Frequency, intensity, time and type. | Use the four variables to make a progression transparent and repeatable. |

## 28. Fault -> Biomechanical Cause -> Intervention

### General principles

| Fault | Likely cause | Coaching / intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Shape breaks under fatigue | Insufficient eccentric strength or poor force absorption | Reduce reps, slow eccentrics and increase quality time under tension. |

## 29. Strength vs Mobility vs Coordination

*A real-time decision framework*

| Category | How it appears | Best first tools |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | The athlete understands or briefly reaches the position but cannot hold or control it. Eccentric and concentric quality differ; shape collapses under load. | Isometrics, eccentrics, tempo, reduced lever with the same shape and positional assistance. |
| Mobility | The athlete cannot physically reach the position even unloaded. Compensations appear immediately and cueing does not change the outcome. | Active mobility, end-range loading, controlled articular work, eccentric lengthening and joint-specific range development. |
| Coordination | The athlete has the range and strength but cannot organise force consistently, especially under speed. | Constraints, pauses, rhythm work, external targets, tactile feedback and reduced speed. |
| Mixed limitation | Most athletes show more than one limiter. | Choose the primary limiter first, then re-test before adding another intervention. |

Final coaching truth: Mobility gives access. Strength gives control. Coordination gives expression. Training the wrong category may make the exercise look productive while progress stalls.

## 30. Scapulothoracic Rhythm and Bodyweight Pressing

*Technical deep dive for Lead Coaches*

Scapulothoracic rhythm describes the coordinated relationship between the glenohumeral joint and the scapula moving on the ribcage. During arm elevation, a simplified teaching ratio is approximately two degrees of humeral movement for every one degree of scapular movement.

The scapula must upwardly rotate, posteriorly tilt and protract or retract at the appropriate time. In push-ups, ring work and handstand push-ups, the scapula is not fixed. It must move and stabilise dynamically while transmitting force between the hand, ribcage, pelvis and the rest of the body.

| Movement phase | Expected scapular behaviour | Common coaching error |
|---|---|---|
| Push-up descent | Controlled retraction and slight downward rotation while the trunk remains organised. | Pinning the shoulder blades so aggressively that natural movement is blocked. |
| Push-up ascent | Protraction led by serratus anterior with the ribcage controlled. | Treating the movement as only chest and triceps work. |
| Handstand / HSPU | Upward rotation and elevation against gravity with midline control. | Using barbell-press cues that fix the scapula or allowing the shoulders to collapse. |
| Ring support | Continuous joint centration and micro-adjustment under instability. | Chasing stillness by muscling the rings rather than stacking and creating torque. |

Teaching synthesis: Barbell pressing teaches athletes to apply force into an object. Bodyweight pressing teaches athletes to organise force through the body. Scapulothoracic rhythm is the bridge between the arm and the trunk.

## 31. Physics and Anatomy Reference

*The concepts most often used during the seminar*

| Concept | Seminar definition | Coaching application |
|---|---|---|
| Biomechanics | The study of movement in living things using the science of mechanics. | Explain what moves, what force creates the movement and where the force should be directed. |
| Newton's first law | A body remains at rest or in motion unless an external force changes its state. | Dynamic apparatus continues to move until the athlete applies a force to redirect or stop it. |
| Newton's second law | Force relates to mass and acceleration (F = m x a). | Greater body mass or acceleration requires greater force and prerequisite strength. |
| Newton's third law | Every action has an equal and opposite reaction. | Press down to rise; do not push equipment away when the intended direction is upward. |
| Centre of mass | The point at which the body's mass is considered concentrated for analysis. | Keep the centre of mass over the base of support in squats, pistols and handstands. |
| Base of support | The area created by contact with the ground or apparatus. | A smaller base increases the balance and coordination demand. |
| Torque | Rotational effect of force: force x moment arm. | Longer levers and poor alignment increase joint demand. |
| Length-tension relationship | Muscle force depends partly on the overlap of contractile filaments at a given length. | Build usable range with strength rather than separating mobility and control. |
| Stretch-shortening cycle | Rapid eccentric loading followed by concentric action can return elastic energy. | Use tension before the change of direction in beat swings, kipping and jumps. |
| Reciprocal inhibition | Activation of an agonist is accompanied by inhibition of its antagonist. | Hypertonic hip flexors may reduce glute contribution and compromise hip opening. |
| Joint stacking | Alignment allows force to travel through bones, connective tissue and active muscle efficiently. | Use supination, elbow lockout, scapular control and trunk alignment in support positions. |

## 32. Spotting Standards

*Scaling, feedback and safety*

| Principle | Lead Coach standard | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Spot the athlete | The coach controls or guides the person, not the equipment. | Lower back and hamstring in strict T2B; fists or hips in handstand; torso and rings in RMU. |
| Preserve the movement intent | Assistance should allow the target position and contraction to remain visible. | Toe spot in pull-ups; supported pistol; guided eccentric RMU. |
| Use tactile information | The spot helps the athlete feel alignment, pressure or force direction. | Band around support position, T-shirt cue on rings, fingertip or fist reference in handstand. |
| Reduce assistance progressively | A spot is not a permanent substitute for strength or coordination. | Move from full guidance to light contact, self-spotting and independent reps. |
| Protect entry and exit | The movement is not safe if access, landing or bailout is uncontrolled. | Boxes for bars, clear ring swing space, safe handstand exits and stable equipment. |
| Communicate before contact | Explain where and why you will touch the athlete and agree a stop signal. | Especially important around hips, lower back, legs and inversion spotting. |

| Consistency | The ability to repeat the intended behaviour or movement quality. | A prerequisite before increasing intensity. |
| Intensity | In CrossFit, commonly related to measurable power: force x distance / time. | Increase output without abandoning range or position. |
| Rule | A principle-based standard that guides safe development and can be adjusted by the coach. | Static before dynamic; strict before kipping. |
| Law | A scientific restriction that cannot be negotiated. | Gravity and Newtonian force relationships. |
| Exercise coaching | Facilitating a programme through personal interventions that increase understanding and execution. | Connect with and advance the athlete, rather than only delivering the task. |
| Athlete / recreational CrossFitter | The course distinguishes professional competitive needs from general-population health and fitness needs. | Use needs analysis rather than copying Games-level training. |

## 36. Lead Coach Final Checklist

*Review before closing the guide*

- [  ] I can explain the purpose and teaching outcome of every module.

- [  ] I can deliver the opening lecture without relying on the complete script.

- [  ] I know the four rules and can connect each one to practical examples.

- [  ] I have assigned demonstrations, spotting and station ownership to the assistant coaches.

- [  ] I can distinguish mobility, strength and coordination limitations in real time.

- [  ] I can preserve the movement intent while scaling lever, load, range, speed or instability.

- [  ] I know the non-negotiable safety points for bar work, rings and inversions.

- [  ] I can close each module with one memorable takeaway and a transition to the next block.

- [  ] I can explain how the weekend methodology changes day-to-day programming.

- [  ] I will complete a team debrief after the seminar and record changes for the next delivery.

Final Lead Coach memory line: The course is not successful because every participant achieves a new skill. It is successful when participants leave able to see movement more clearly, make safer decisions and build better long-term development processes.
